Basal Cell Carcinoma (BCC)

Basal cell carcinoma is the most common form of skin cancer and the most common cancer overall in humans. It arises from the basal cells in the lowest layer of the epidermis. BCC grows slowly and rarely spreads (metastasizes) to other parts of the body, but if left untreated it can invade and destroy surrounding tissue, including cartilage and bone, causing significant local damage.

Cause and risk factors: cumulative, long-term ultraviolet (UV) exposure from sunlight or tanning beds is the main cause. Risk is higher in people with fair skin, light hair and eyes, older age, chronic sun exposure, a history of sunburn, radiation exposure, and weakened immune systems. Most lesions appear on sun-exposed areas such as the face, ears, neck, scalp, and shoulders.

Appearance: BCC can look like a pearly or waxy bump, sometimes with visible small blood vessels (telangiectasia); a flat, scaly, flesh-colored or brown patch; a sore that bleeds, oozes, or crusts and does not heal or heals and returns; or a white, scar-like area. It may be shiny and translucent.

Diagnosis is confirmed by skin biopsy. Treatment options include surgical excision, Mohs micrographic surgery (especially on the face for high cure rates and tissue conservation), curettage and electrodesiccation, cryotherapy, topical medications (imiquimod, 5-fluorouracil) for superficial lesions, and radiation therapy. Cure rates are very high when treated early.

Prevention: sun protection with broad-spectrum sunscreen, protective clothing, shade, avoidance of tanning beds, and regular skin checks.
