Dermatofibroma (DF)

Dermatofibroma is a common benign (non-cancerous) skin nodule made up of fibrous tissue. It is harmless and does not turn into cancer. Dermatofibromas are most often found on the legs, especially in adult women, but can occur anywhere on the body.

Cause: the exact cause is unknown, but dermatofibromas frequently develop after minor skin trauma such as an insect bite, a splinter, or a shaving nick, suggesting a reactive fibrous overgrowth rather than a true tumor.

Appearance: a dermatofibroma is a small, firm, raised nodule, usually 3 to 10 mm across. Color ranges from pink and flesh-toned to reddish-brown or dark brown. A characteristic and useful diagnostic feature is the "dimple sign" (or Fitzpatrick sign): when the skin on either side of the lesion is pinched, the nodule dimples inward rather than protruding, because it is tethered to the deeper tissue. On dermoscopy a central white scar-like area with a surrounding pigment network is typical.

Symptoms: usually none. Some dermatofibromas are tender, itchy, or sensitive to touch or pressure.

Management: no treatment is required. The lesion is benign and persistent, often lasting for years or indefinitely. Surgical excision can be performed if the lesion is symptomatic, cosmetically bothersome, or if the diagnosis is uncertain, but excision may leave a scar and lesions can recur. Any nodule that grows rapidly, changes markedly, or bleeds should be evaluated to exclude other diagnoses.
